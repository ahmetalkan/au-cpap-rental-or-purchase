Rent or buy a CPAP in Australia? A worksheet for after the study

Once a sleep study has confirmed obstructive sleep apnoea and a prescription is in hand, the next fork is practical: rent a machine for a trial, or buy one from a registered supplier. This worksheet lays out what each path actually requires, using Australian clinic guides and official health pages — not a sales script.

Healthdirect describes obstructive sleep apnoea by how many apnoeas occur per hour: mild 5 to 14, and it notes that a doctor may recommend an oral appliance for mild or moderate OSA and CPAP for severe OSA. CPAP, on that page, is a pump that feeds pressurised air into a mask worn at night. Victoria’s Better Health Channel uses a similar hourly guide (normal fewer than 5 interruptions, mild 5–15, moderate 15–30, severe more than 30) and estimates that about 5% of Australians have sleep apnoea, with around 1 in 4 men over 30 affected. The Sleep Health Foundation’s public site puts better sleep as a national health priority. Those figures explain why a machine decision is common; they do not decide whether you should rent or buy.

Start with the prescription, not the shop

A guide to CPAP prescriptions in Australia calls the prescription a written authorisation from a sleep specialist or respiratory physician that lets a patient obtain CPAP equipment. A diagnostic sleep study is normally required first. The prescription typically specifies device type, a pressure range or a fixed pressure, humidifier needs and a recommended mask. Patients generally need that document to claim Medicare rebates or private health insurance benefits for devices and accessories.

Only after that paper exists does retail or rental make sense. The page on how CPAP retail works in Australia defines retail as buying a machine, mask and accessories from a registered supplier after a study confirms OSA, through sleep centres, pharmacies and specialist CPAP suppliers.

What rental is for

The companion article on CPAP rental in Australia says you can rent from sleep clinics, dedicated CPAP suppliers and some respiratory or home-healthcare providers. Importantly, it also says Medicare does not usually pay for CPAP machine rental directly. Rental is therefore typically a self-funded or fund-supported trial: a way to test mask comfort and nightly use before a purchase, not a Medicare-funded long-term substitute.

Decision worksheet

QuestionYour note
Do I have a written CPAP prescription (device type, pressure, humidifier, mask)?
What did the study report as the hourly interruption / AHI band (mild, moderate, severe)?
Has the clinician recommended CPAP, an oral appliance, or a trial of either?
Will my private fund contribute to a purchase, and what invoice do they need?
Am I renting to test a mask and adherence, and for how many weeks is the quote?
If I rent, what is the weekly or monthly fee, and does any of it credit toward a later purchase?
If I buy, is the supplier registered, and what is the warranty and mask-fitting follow-up?

If the prescription is not yet issued, stop and finish the study pathway first. If funds will not contribute to rental but will contribute to a purchase, a short paid rental still may be cheaper than buying a mask style you cannot wear — that arithmetic is yours to do with the quotes in hand, not a number invented here.

When you are ready to compare clinics and suppliers, Sleep Clinics AU lists 907 Australian clinics and explains how Medicare and health funds change what you pay for a study.

Official pages describe CPAP as a treatment a doctor may recommend, especially for more severe OSA. This worksheet does not tell you to start, stop or skip prescribed therapy.
